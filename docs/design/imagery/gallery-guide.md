# Photographing originals — a guide for the gallery

*Bahasa Indonesia:* [gallery-guide.id.md](gallery-guide.id.md)

For the owner and staff of Indies Gallery. It explains how to photograph a map, a
print or a photograph so that a collector on the other side of the world can judge
it as if it were in their hands. You do not need to be a photographer, and you never
need to edit a photo: take it well, send the original file, and we do the rest.

The shot list for each kind of object is one page: [map](shot-lists/map.md) ·
[print](shot-lists/print.md) · [photograph](shot-lists/photograph.md).

## 1. What a good photograph of an original looks like

- **The whole sheet**, edges and all, with a little background around it.
- **Square on** — the camera straight above, so the edges are straight lines.
- **Even light** — no bright patch, no dark corner, no shadow along one edge.
- **True colour** — a colour card and a ruler lie beside the sheet in every photo.
- **Nothing hidden, nothing fixed** — stains, tears and repairs show exactly as they
  are. The photo and the condition report must agree.
- **No people and no hands** in the frame. The object alone.

## 2. What you need — good, better, best

Start with what you have. "Good" is enough for the website; "better" and "best"
make larger prints possible for Old East Indies and make condition easier to judge.

| | Good | Better | Best |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Camera | a phone from the last few years, its main (1×) camera | a mirrorless or DSLR camera, 20 megapixels or more, a 50 mm or macro lens | the same, tethered to a laptop so you see each shot large |
| Holding it | a phone stand or tripod with an arm that points straight down | a tripod with a sideways arm, or a copy stand | a copy stand |
| Light | daylight from a window, indirect — never sun on the sheet | two identical daylight lamps (5000–5600 K, "CRI 95" or higher) | the two lamps with polarising filters, and one on the lens |
| Colour reference | an 18% grey card and a steel ruler | a small 24-patch colour chart and a ruler | a full-size colour chart and a ruler |
| Background | a matte dark grey or black board or cloth | the same, always the same one | the same |
| Extras | — | a second, single lamp for raking light; an LED light pad for transmitted light | a remote release; a soft brush and a rubber air blower for dust |

A 24-patch chart means, for example, a Calibrite (formerly X-Rite) ColorChecker
Classic Mini or Passport. Any real colour chart works; a printed copy of one does not.

## 3. How big a sheet your camera can take in one photo

For Old East Indies to print a reproduction at the original's own size, the photo
needs about **240 pixels for every inch of the sheet** (240 ppi, D26). The website
itself needs much less — any recent phone is enough for it. Roughly, leaving a
margin around the sheet:

| Camera | Largest sheet at 240 ppi, in one photo |
| ------ | ------------------------------------- |
| a 12-megapixel phone | about 36 cm on the long side |
| a 24-megapixel camera | about 54 cm |
| a 45-megapixel camera | about 73 cm |
| a phone with a 48-megapixel mode | about 72 cm on paper — in practice less sharp than the number suggests |

A larger sheet photographed in one frame is still fine for the website; it just
limits the largest print. If a big map matters for prints, tell us: it can be
photographed in overlapping sections and joined, and we will say how first.

## 4. Setting up the table

1. A clean, dry, steady table, away from food, drink and fans.
2. Lay the background down, flat. It should be bigger than the sheet by at least
   5 cm on every side. Use the same background for every item.
3. **Handle the sheet with clean, dry hands** (or nitrile gloves). Lift it by two
   corners, with a card underneath if it is fragile.
4. **Nothing touches the printed area.** No tape, no pins, no glass pressed on it.
   If a sheet curls, soft weights on the blank margins only; if it will not lie flat
   safely, photograph it as it lies — a curl is honest, a torn corner is not.
5. Put the **colour card and the ruler beside the sheet**, on the background, not on
   the sheet: same light, same height, fully visible, not overlapping it.
6. Blow loose dust off the sheet and the background before each photo.

## 5. Light and colour

**Use one kind of light at a time.** Daylight and a warm ceiling bulb together give
colours that cannot be corrected. Switch the room lights off.

**Good — a window.** Indirect daylight: a window with no sun coming through it, or an
overcast day. Put the table near the window, and a white board or sheet of foam
board on the far side to bounce light back, so the side away from the window is not
darker. Shoot within a steady hour of the day, not at sunset.

**Better — two lamps.** Two identical daylight lamps (5000–5600 K), one on each side,
each pointing at the sheet's centre from about 45°, each at least one and a half
times the sheet's length away. Equal distance on both sides. This is the classic
setup for flat art, and it gives even light with no shadow.

**Best — polarised.** A polarising film on each lamp and a polarising filter on the
lens, turned until the shine disappears. It removes glare from glossy photographs,
varnish, gold and glass.

**Never direct sun on an original** — the light and heat fade hand colouring and
harm photographs. (The shop's products are another matter: see the shop guide.)

**Check your light before the first item.** Put a plain grey card or board where the
sheet will go and take a photo. The corners should look as bright as the middle. If
one side is darker, move the lamp or the reflector until they match.

**White balance.** On a camera, set it from the grey card (custom white balance), or
shoot RAW. On a phone, automatic is fine — the card in the frame lets us correct it.
Never add a filter or a "warm" look.

## 6. The camera

- **Straight above, parallel to the sheet.** On a phone, turn on the grid; many phones
  show a level when pointing straight down. The sheet's edges should be straight
  and parallel to the frame's edges.
- **Fill most of the frame** with the sheet — about four-fifths — with the colour card
  and ruler in the remaining space. Use the main camera (1×) and move it; never zoom
  digitally, never use the wide 0.5× lens.
- **Keep it still.** Stand or tripod, and the 2-second timer or a remote, so pressing
  the button does not shake it.
- **Focus on the sheet** (tap on it) and check a photo at full size: the finest lines
  and the smallest text should be sharp in the centre and in the corners.
- **Phone settings:** the highest resolution the phone offers; RAW or "Pro" mode if it
  has one (iPhone Pro: ProRAW; many Android phones: Pro mode with RAW or DNG);
  switch off filters, portrait mode, beauty modes and "scene optimiser". Flash off.
- **Camera settings:** RAW (plus JPEG if you like), lowest ISO, aperture around f/8,
  lens corrections on, timer or remote.
- **Exposure:** the white patch on the card must still show detail, not a blown-out
  white. If the paper looks glaring white, the photo is too bright.

## 7. Raking light — to show the surface

One light only, **low and from the side**, so that every plate mark, fold, crease,
repair and bit of cockling throws a small shadow. It is how collectors check an
engraving's plate mark and how a hidden repair shows itself.

- All other lights off (a darkened room, or curtains closed).
- One lamp at the side of the sheet, **10–20° above the table** — for a lamp one metre
  away, the bulb is about 18 to 36 cm above the table top. On the "good" tier, a
  second phone's torch held low at the side does the job.
- Light from the left side of the sheet, then, if the sheet has folds running the
  other way, a second photo lit from the top.
- The colour card stays in the frame, even though the light is uneven.

## 8. Transmitted light — to see through the paper (better tier)

Light from behind shows watermarks, chain lines, thin spots, tears and old repairs.

- An LED light pad (a tracing pad) under the sheet; front lights off. LED pads stay
  cool — never use a window with sun on it, never a hot lamp.
- Only for loose sheets in good enough condition to be moved onto the pad.
  **Never for photographs on mounts**, never for anything that might be stressed.
- Keep it brief. The ruler goes in the frame; the colour card is not needed here.

## 9. Frames, mats and glass

- **If an item is sold in its frame or mat**, photograph it as it will be delivered
  (the `framed` shot): the whole frame, square on, lit from both sides at 45° so the
  glass does not reflect the room, the lamps or you. A polarising filter helps.
- **The recto is always the sheet itself.** If it can come out of the mat or frame
  safely, take it out for the recto, verso and details. If it cannot — or you would
  rather not — say so in your notes. We then mark the photos "photographed in its
  mat", because a mat hides the margins and a collector must know that.
- Never photograph a recto through glass if the sheet can come out.

## 10. The mat and the shadow — we add them, you don't

On the website each sheet sits on a digital mat with a soft shadow under it, the
same for every item. That is why the photo must have **no shadow of its own** (even
light on both sides) and **a clean, even background** around the sheet. We crop the
photo to the sheet with a thin margin, or cut the sheet out along its real edge —
torn corners and deckle edges included. We never crop into the sheet.

## 11. What never to do to a photo of an original

Never remove, soften or "clean up" a stain, a spot of foxing, a tear, a hole, a
repair, a fold, fading or a trimmed margin — not with an app, not with a filter,
not with "enhance". If dust is on the sheet, blow it off and take the photo again.
The raking and transmitted-light photos are there to show defects — a repair that only
shows in raking light is exactly what they are for.

The shop may restore its **reproductions** — that is a separate copy, labelled and
disclosed — but a photograph of the original is never restored, on either site. The
full rules are in [retouching-and-labelling.md](retouching-and-labelling.md).

## 12. After the shoot

1. Look at each photo at full size: sharp, even, the card visible, nothing cut off.
2. Keep the **original files** from the camera or phone — not edited copies.
3. Name and send them as [handover.md](handover.md) explains — through the shared
   folder, **never through WhatsApp or email**, which shrink the photos.

We check every image and reply with a result for each: pass, fix, or re-take, and
exactly why ([intake-spec.md](intake-spec.md)).
